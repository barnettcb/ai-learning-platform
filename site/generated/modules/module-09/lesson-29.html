How Do I Choose the Right AI Approach?

What you will be able to do

You can match the task to an appropriate combination of conversation, search, uploaded material, visual input, or non-AI work.

The key idea

Choose the approach from the information the task requires, not from the feature that seems most impressive.

Ask four questions:

  1. What result am I trying to produce?
  2. Where must the supporting information come from?
  3. How current, exact, or sensitive is that information?
  4. What level of human review is required?

The answers determine whether AI should work from general knowledge, current sources, supplied files, images, or no AI at all.

Common approaches

Conversation only

Use for brainstorming, rewriting supplied text, practicing explanations, and developing ideas that do not depend on current or private facts.

Web-connected research

Use when the task depends on recent events, changing prices, current rules, schedules, product details, or source attribution.

Uploaded files

Use when the answer must be grounded in a specific document, table, report, policy, or collection of notes.

Image input

Use when relevant information appears in a photograph, screenshot, diagram, or visual layout.

Human or specialist work

Use when the task requires access, authority, physical inspection, professional diagnosis, confidential judgment, or accountability that AI cannot provide.

Several approaches may be combined, but each should have a clear purpose.

Example

A learner wants help understanding a new employment policy.

A poor approach is to ask a general chatbot what the policy “probably means.”

A stronger approach is to upload the actual policy, ask for a section-by-section explanation, identify ambiguous language, and bring unresolved legal or workplace questions to an authorized person.

Match the tool to the evidence

Use this practical rule:

  • general task with no external facts → conversation may be enough;
  • changing factual question → search current sources;
  • document-specific question → provide the document;
  • visual question → provide the image;
  • high-stakes judgment → use AI only in a supporting role.

Best practice State the evidence boundary in the request: “Use only the attached file,” “Use current official sources,” or “Treat this as brainstorming rather than factual research.”

Watch for this The learner uses ordinary conversation for a task that depends on unavailable or current information. AI fills the gap with plausible language, and the result is mistaken for researched fact.

Try it

Choose an approach for each task:

  • revise a paragraph already written;
  • compare current airline baggage rules;
  • summarize a lease;
  • identify controls visible in an appliance photo;
  • decide whether chest pain requires urgent care.

The final task requires professional or emergency guidance rather than AI judgment.

Apply it

Take one real task and write a short approach statement identifying the inputs, tools, evidence boundary, and human review needed before you begin.

Check yourself

You are ready to continue when you can justify the chosen approach based on the task's evidence and risk requirements.
